# Company Research: Decide Before You Apply

An application takes 30-45 minutes. A few minutes of research up front decides whether those 45 minutes are an investment or a waste.

---

## Most job seekers research after they get an interview

Most job seekers research *after* they get an interview. By then they have already paid the cost — the tailored resume, the cover letter, the application form, the days of low-grade hope while they wait.

The cost doesn't stop at the application, either. In Employ's 2026 Job Seeker Nation Report, the most common reason people gave for leaving a job within 90 days was "a mismatch between what was promised during the hiring process and the reality of the role" — 46%, up ten points year over year. Most of that mismatch is discoverable from the outside, before you apply.

> Spend a few minutes deciding if you want this job *before* spending a lot of minutes convincing them to give it to you.

---

## What You Are Actually Looking For

Three things, in roughly this order:

**Does the role exist as advertised?** Make sure you're sourcing job postings from legitimate sources. If you find a job post in the wild that you're not sure about, check the company's career page first.

**Does the company match the posting?** Look at recent news, funding state, layoffs, and the careers page. A company that just cut 20% of staff and is now "aggressively hiring" is telling you something. So is a company whose careers page describes a culture that contradicts everything in the role description.

**For remote or hybrid roles, is the work model real?** "Remote" is the most abused word in tech hiring. The same label covers fully distributed companies and city-first offices that allow Wednesdays from home. The five-tier model in [Remotivated's work model guide](https://remotivated.com/resources/work-models?utm_source=github&utm_medium=repo&utm_campaign=job-hunt-skills&utm_content=guide) — Fully Remote, Remote-First, Flexible Hybrid, Structured Hybrid, Onsite — is a useful frame for figuring out which one you are actually looking at.

## A Two-Minute Scam Screen

The first of those three questions — does the role exist as advertised? — has a checkable answer, and it is worth spending two minutes on before the other forty-three.

In Employ's 2026 Job Seeker Nation Report, **53% of jobseekers said they had come across a job posting they believed was a scam**. Read that as an encounter rate over a career, not a share of postings: the overwhelming majority of postings are real, and about half of us have run into one that wasn't. The useful part is that the same survey asked what gave it away, and the answers are all things you can check from the posting itself, in minutes, before you write a word of a cover letter.

The red flags candidates named, and the share who named each:

- **The role seems too good to be true (50%).** Pay well above the band for the title, seniority that doesn't match the requirements, a hiring process with no steps in it.
- **A request for payment (48%).** For equipment, training, onboarding software, a background check, anything. Employers pay to hire you. This one has no legitimate version.
- **Being asked for personal identity information too early (43%).** More on the timing below, because this is the flag people most often get wrong in both directions.
- **The company could not be verified (40%).** The one this guide is built to answer.
- **A poorly written or inconsistent job description (36%).** Not a typo — inconsistency. A title that doesn't match the duties, a location that contradicts the work model, a salary band that contradicts the seniority.
- **Communication through unofficial channels (35%).** Texting or WhatsApp from an unknown number, a personal Gmail address, an interview conducted entirely over chat. Legitimate recruiters send mail from a domain you can look up.

**Verification is the flag you can actually resolve.** A real employer has a footprint that exists independently of the posting: a careers page on their own domain that lists this role, employees on LinkedIn whose profiles predate the ad, news coverage, funding or registration records, a business address. If the only evidence the company exists is the posting and the person messaging you about it, you have your answer. Directories that verify remote employers shortcut the check — [Remotivated](https://remotivated.com?utm_source=job-hunt-skills&utm_medium=guide&utm_campaign=company-research) does exactly this — but the manual version is five minutes of the same work described in **Where to Look** below.

**On identity verification, the tell is timing, not the ask.** Being asked to prove who you are is not a scam signal by itself, and most candidates are comfortable with it: in the same survey, 50% said they were very comfortable with identity verification and 33% somewhat comfortable, while only 8% said they would abandon an application if asked to verify upfront. Employ's own summary of the finding is "The challenge isn't willingness, it's timing."

Timing is where the signal actually lives. **31% of candidates reported being asked to verify their identity before even applying.** Legitimate employers tend to verify late — at offer, at background check, at onboarding — because that is the point at which they have a reason to. So the rule isn't "never verify". It's that an identity request arriving before any human contact is a red flag, not a formality. In practice that means: no Social Security number, date of birth, passport or licence scan, or bank details before you have spoken with a named person at the company, on a channel that traces back to its domain.

None of this requires suspicion as a default posture. It requires reading — the same skill the [ATS myths guide](ats-myths.md) asks for when it tells you to read every application question before you answer it. The questions you answer and the details you hand over are the two places in an application where careless reading actually costs you something.

## Where to Look

- **The job posting itself.** Most red flags are already in there.
- **The company's careers page and recent news.** Funding, layoffs, leadership changes, product direction.
- **Glassdoor, Blind, Reddit.** Looking for *repeated patterns*, not individual angry reviews. Many people describing the same failure mode is evidence; one is noise.
- **LinkedIn.** Where employees in the relevant function actually live, how long people in similar roles stay, and whether leadership is concentrated in one office.
- **The [GitLab All-Remote Handbook](https://handbook.gitlab.com/handbook/company/culture/all-remote/guide/#how-to-evaluate-a-remote-role).** Public, opinionated, and useful as a checklist for evaluating remote roles specifically.
